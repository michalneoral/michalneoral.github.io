We estimate a few parameters instead of the whole density, e.g. \(\cth = (\mu, \sigma^2)\) of a normal distribution.

The setting

Known form, unknown parameters: \[ \cp(\cx \mid \ck) = \cp(\cx \mid \ck, \cth_\ck) \] Each class separately, as with Parzen: we drop \(\ck\) and write \(\cp(\cx \mid \cth)\).

Training set, samples i.i.d.: \[ \cT = \{\cx_1, \cx_2, \dots, \cx_N\} \]

Likelihood of the parameters: \[ L(\cth) = \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth) \]

Log-likelihood, same maximum: \[ \ell(\cth) = \ln L(\cth) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth) \]

\(L(\cth)\) is a function of \(\cth\) for fixed data, not a distribution over \(\cth\).

Three ways to estimate \(\cth\)

Maximum likelihood (ML)

\(\cT = \{\cx_1, \dots, \cx_N\}\) training set, \(\cx_i\) i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) \(\;\Rightarrow\; \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth)\)

Choose the parameters under which the data are most probable:

\[ \hML{\cth} = \arg\max_{\cth} \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \arg\max_{\cth} \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth) \]

Prediction for a new \(\cx^*\): plug in the estimate, \(\;\cp(\cx^* \mid \cT) \approx \cp(\cx^* \mid \hML{\cth})\)

Result: \(\hML{\cth}\), one number (a point estimate), not a distribution.

Pros

Simple, no extra assumptions, the best with a lot of data.

Cons

With few data it trusts them blindly: 3 tosses, 3 heads, so \(\hat\pi_\text{head} = 1\)?

Maximum a posteriori (MAP)

\(\cT = \{\cx_1, \dots, \cx_N\}\) training set, \(\cx_i\) i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) \(\;\Rightarrow\; \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth)\)

\(\cth\) is a random variable with a prior \(\cp(\cth)\). Maximize the posterior:

\[ \hMAP{\cth} = \arg\max_{\cth} \cp(\cth \mid \cT) = \arg\max_{\cth} \frac{\cp(\cT \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth)}{\cp(\cT)} = \arg\max_{\cth} \cp(\cT \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth) \]

  • \(\cp(\cT)\) does not depend on \(\cth\): drop it
  • the prior \(\cp(\cth)\) has to be specified

Result: \(\hMAP{\cth}\), again one number.

Convenient prior: conjugate. The posterior has the same form as the prior.

Prior, likelihood, posterior

Before the data: the prior \(\cp(\cth)\).

The data: likelihood \(\cp(\cT \mid \cth)\), its maximum is \(\hML{\cth}\).

Posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior: a compromise between the two.

Bayesian inference: keep the whole posterior

\(\cT = \{\cx_1, \dots, \cx_N\}\) training set, \(\cx_i\) i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) \(\;\Rightarrow\; \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth)\)

Do not pick one \(\cth\), keep the whole distribution: \[ \cp(\cth \mid \cT) = \frac{\cp(\cT \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth)}{\cp(\cT)} \]

Prediction: average all models, weighted by their probability: \[ \cp(\cx^* \mid \cT) = \int \cp(\cx^* \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, d\cth \]

Result: a distribution \(\cp(\cth \mid \cT)\), no single \(\cth\). The prediction is a distribution too.

Pros

Uses all the information, knows how uncertain it is.

Cons

The integral: in closed form only for conjugate priors.

Comparing the estimates

\(N = 4\) samples, normal model, unknown \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\). The prior puts \(\mu\) around \(\mu_0 = 3\).

ML: plug in \(\hML{\cth}\). It fits the 4 samples tightly, too narrow.

MAP: the prior pulls \(\mu\) towards \(\mu_0\) and makes \(\sigma\) larger.

Bayes: the predictive \(\cp(\cx^* \mid \cT)\), not a normal any more.

It averages models from \(\cp(\cth \mid \cT)\): 10 of them drawn, one highlighted.

Bayesian parameter estimation (MMSE)

\(\cT = \{\cx_1, \dots, \cx_N\}\) training set, \(\cx_i\) i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) \(\;\Rightarrow\; \cp(\cT \mid \cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \cp(\cx_i \mid \cth)\)

Or: pick one \(\hat\cth\) with the smallest expected squared error: \[ R(\hat\cth) = \int \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, (\cth - \hat\cth)^2\, d\cth \]

\[ \frac{\partial R}{\partial \hat\cth} = -2 \int \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, (\cth - \hat\cth)\, d\cth = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hMMSE{\cth} = \int \cth\, \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, d\cth \]

Result: \(\hMMSE{\cth} = \mathbb{E}[\cth \mid \cT]\), one number: the mean of the posterior. Prediction by plugging in.

Comparing the estimates: MMSE

ML, MAP and Bayes from before.

MMSE: plug in the posterior mean. Still a normal, unlike Bayes.

Three estimates, one picture

prior result prediction \(\cp(\cx^* \mid \cT)\)
ML no \(\hML{\cth}\), max of likelihood \(\cp(\cx^* \mid \hML{\cth})\)
MAP yes \(\hMAP{\cth}\), max of posterior \(\cp(\cx^* \mid \hMAP{\cth})\)
MMSE yes \(\hMMSE{\cth}\), mean of posterior \(\cp(\cx^* \mid \hMMSE{\cth})\)
Bayes yes the whole \(\cp(\cth \mid \cT)\) \(\int \cp(\cx^* \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, d\cth\)

With a flat prior, MAP equals ML.

With a lot of data, all four agree.

Conjugate priors

likelihood \(\cp(\cx \mid \cth)\) parameter conjugate prior posterior
binomial \(\cpi\) Beta \((a, b)\) Beta \((a + R,\; b + N - R)\)
categorical \(p_1, \dots, p_K\) Dirichlet \((\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)\) Dirichlet \((\alpha_k + N_k)\)
normal \(\mu\) (\(\sigma^2\) known) normal \((\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) normal
normal \(\mu, \sigma^2\) NIG NIG assignment
Poisson \(\lambda\) Gamma \((\alpha, \beta)\) Gamma \((\alpha + \sum \cx_i,\; \beta + N)\)
uniform \([0, \cth]\) \(\cth\) Pareto \((\cth_0, k)\) Pareto \((\max(\cth_0, m),\; k + N)\)

The full table: Wikipedia, Conjugate prior.

The German tank problem

Counting tanks from serial numbers

WW2: the Allies read serial numbers on captured tanks (gearboxes, wheels). How many tanks does Germany produce per month?

month intelligence statistics German records
June 1940 1000 169 122
June 1941 1550 244 271
August 1942 1550 327 342

Ruggles and Brodie (1947); table from Wikipedia, German tank problem.

We have seen \(n\) tanks, the largest serial number is \(m\). How many tanks are there?

The model: uniform distribution

Serial numbers are uniform on \([0, \cth]\), \(\cth\) is the number of tanks: \[ \cp(\cx \mid \cth) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\cth} & 0 \le \cx \le \cth\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] Continuous for simplicity, real serial numbers are integers.

\[ L(\cth) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\cth} [\![\cx_i \le \cth]\!] \] \[ L(\cth) = \begin{cases} \cth^{-n} & \cth \ge \max_i \cx_i\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

\[ \hML{\cth} = \max_i \cx_i = m \]

Is it a good estimate? Can the true \(\cth\) be smaller than \(m\)?

ML with more and more tanks

Derivation: Bayes for the tanks

Prior: \(\cth\) uniform on \([0, M]\), at most \(M\) tanks. Posterior, \(m = \max_i \cx_i\): \[ \cp(\cth \mid \cT) \propto \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\cth} [\![\cx_i \le \cth]\!]\; \frac{1}{M} [\![\cth \le M]\!] \quad\Rightarrow\quad \cp(\cth \mid \cT) = \frac{1}{Z} \frac{1}{\cth^{n}} [\![m \le \cth \le M]\!] \]

\[ Z = \int_m^M \frac{d\cth}{\cth^{n}} = \frac{1}{n - 1} \left( \frac{1}{m^{n-1}} - \frac{1}{M^{n-1}} \right), \qquad \hMAP{\cth} = m = \hML{\cth} \;\;\text{(flat prior)} \]

\[ \hMMSE{\cth} = \frac{1}{Z} \int_m^M \frac{\cth}{\cth^{n}}\, d\cth \;\xrightarrow{M \to \infty}\; m\, \frac{n - 1}{n - 2}, \qquad \cp(\cx \mid \cT) = \int_{\max(\cx, m)}^{M} \frac{1}{\cth}\, \cp(\cth \mid \cT)\, d\cth \]

ML, MAP, MMSE and Bayes for the tanks

One tank, \(\cx_1 = 5.15\): the ML plug-in density ends at the observation.

Posterior over \(\cth\) (prior uniform on \([0, 10]\)): its maximum is again \(m\).

Its mean \(\hMMSE{\cth} = 7.3\) lies to the right of \(m\).

Bayesian prediction \(\cp(\cx \mid \cT)\): larger numbers are still possible.

Ten tanks: the posterior is narrow, all estimates are close.

The real (discrete) problem

Serial numbers \(1, 2, \dots, N\), we see \(k\) of them, the largest is \(m\):

estimate formula \(k = 4\), \(m = 60\)
ML \(m\) 60
unbiased \(m + \dfrac{m}{k} - 1\) 74
Bayes, posterior mean \(\dfrac{(m - 1)(k - 1)}{k - 2}\) 89

ML is biased: it always underestimates. Adding the average gap \(\frac{m}{k}\) fixes it.

Why is ML the most common?

Why ML?

Under general conditions, as \(N \to \infty\)

  • consistent: converges to the true \(\cth\)
  • asymptotically unbiased and efficient (the smallest variance)
  • asymptotically normal

In practice

  • no prior to choose
  • a lot of data: MAP and Bayes give the same result
  • Bayes needs integrals: closed form only for conjugate priors

But with few data

  • overconfident: tanks (and socks, unseen categories later)
  • biased: \(\hat\sigma^2_\text{ML} = \frac{1}{N} \sum (\cx_i - \hat\mu)^2\), \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2_\text{ML}] = \frac{N-1}{N} \sigma^2\)

A bit of history

  • 1769 Lagrange: ML for the multinomial gives relative frequencies
  • 1778 D. Bernoulli: multiply the densities of the observations, maximize
  • 1809 Gauss: posterior mode with a flat prior, gives least squares
  • 1912, 1922 Fisher: the method gets its name and its theory

"Who would rise to argue in favor of a method of minimum likelihood, or even mediocre likelihood?"

S. M. Stigler: The Epic Story of Maximum Likelihood, Statistical Science, 2007.

A die with \(K\) sides

\[ \cp(\cx = k \mid p_1, \dots, p_K) = p_k \]

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k = 1, \qquad N_k = \text{count of } k \]

  2. Find \(\hat p_k^\text{ML}\). Do not forget the constraint!

Hint: use a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint \(\sum_k p_k = 1\).

Waiting times \(\cT = \{t_1, \dots, t_N\}\), exponential distribution: \[ \cp(t \mid \lambda) = \frac{1}{\lambda}\, e^{-t / \lambda}, \qquad t \ge 0 \] \(\lambda\): the mean waiting time

Try it yourself, then check your result:

\(\hML{\lambda} = \dfrac{1}{N} \sum_i t_i, \qquad \hML{\cth} = \dfrac{1}{\hML{\lambda}}\)

  1. Find \(\hML{\lambda}\).
  2. Substitute \(\cth = 1 / \lambda\) and find \(\hML{\cth}\). What is the relation?

Take-away for the lab

  • Known form \(\cp(\cx \mid \cth)\): estimate the parameters from i.i.d. samples
  • ML: \(\arg\max_\cth \cp(\cT \mid \cth)\), simple, the best with a lot of data
  • MAP: \(\arg\max_\cth \cp(\cT \mid \cth)\, \cp(\cth)\), the prior as virtual observations
  • Bayes: the whole posterior, prediction averages all models
  • MMSE: the mean of the posterior
  • With few data the choice matters: socks, unseen categories, German tanks
